Primary laboratory and chemistry findings

Hatfield & Valdes (1978) — historically identified Gymnopilus collections

Analytical detection of psilocybin was reported in specimens then identified as G. spectabilis and certain other Gymnopilus species. This did not establish consistent presence in all geographic collections, modern taxonomic identity, or a standardized human exposure profile.

PubMed · PMID 565861
Kusano and colleagues (1986) — contrasting Japanese collection

The chemical-constituent study reported no detected psilocybin or related compounds in its examined G. spectabilis material, documenting an important geographic/collection and identification caveat.

Chemical and Pharmaceutical Bulletin · DOI 10.1248/cpb.34.3465
Gymnopilins — nicotinic receptor experiments (2014)

Cellular studies examined gymnopilin-related inhibition of nicotinic acetylcholine receptor responses. These are in-vitro pharmacology findings, not controlled demonstrations of subjective effects or toxicity in humans.

Toxicon · Gymnopilins and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors

Controlled human trials — purified psilocybin (comparison only)

Goodwin et al. (2022) — phase-2 treatment-resistant depression trial

233 participants received varying controlled amounts of investigational purified psilocybin with clinical support. The study reported short-term changes in depressive symptom scores and adverse events that included headache, nausea, dizziness, suicidal ideation or behavior, and self-injury in study groups. It is not evidence that this wild specimen treats depression.

New England Journal of Medicine · DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2206443
Carhart-Harris et al. (2021) — psilocybin versus escitalopram trial

A controlled comparison of purified psilocybin and escitalopram reported psychological outcomes and treatment-emergent effects; headache was frequently recorded. Results concern supervised pharmaceutical research, not foraged fungi.

New England Journal of Medicine · DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2032994

Stage 1

Quantitative integrity: A 2014 PC12-cell assay reported approximately 50% suppression of an acetylcholine-evoked calcium signal. That is neither a mushroom compound percentage nor a human adverse-event rate. The occurrence and concentration of psilocybin or psilocin in these photographed mushrooms remain unverified.

Taxonomy: Gymnopilus spectabilis historically applied within the G. junonius complex. Attribution to a modern taxon requires morphology plus voucher and, preferably, molecular analysis. This is a research reconstruction, never an identification authority.

Research evidence ledger — primary studies
  1. Hatfield, Valdes & Smith (1978), Lloydia 41:140–144. PMID 565861. Psilocybin detected in screened G. spectabilis material. Assay detection capability: 0.0004% dry weight; NOT measured species concentration. The cited 0.12% isolation yield belongs to G. validipes, NOT G. spectabilis.
  2. Kusano, Koike, Inoue & Nozoe (1986), Chemical & Pharmaceutical Bulletin 34:3465–3470. DOI. Did NOT detect psilocybin-related compounds in their collections; reported cerevisterol, an acetylenic triol, ergosterol, ergosteryl peroxide and choline.
  3. Kayano et al. (2014), Toxicon 81:23–31. DOI. Gymnopilins isolated from Japanese G. junonius inhibited nicotinic acetylcholine receptor signaling in PC12 cells; acetylcholine-evoked intracellular Ca²⁺ response fell by approximately 50% under tested conditions. They also inhibited nicotine/DMPP responses, without suppressing tested voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel currents.
Mechanism A — serotonergic (conditional on analytical psilocybin confirmation)

Psilocybin → phosphatases → psilocin → 5-HT2A receptor → Gq/11 → phospholipase C-β → IP3 + DAG → intracellular calcium release and protein kinase C signaling → altered cortical network activity. This is a general psilocin mechanism, not evidence that any specific collected mushroom contains psilocybin.

Mechanism B — gymnopilin pharmacology (cultured-cell evidence)

Gymnopilin-containing fraction → nicotinic acetylcholine receptor inhibition → diminished agonist-evoked membrane currents / downstream calcium responses. Exact human dose-response, absorption and clinical effect size are unestablished. Do not conflate nAChR inhibition with 5-HT2A agonism.

Constituents and numerical evidence

Psilocybin: reported in some identified samples, absent in others; reliable universal dry-weight percentage: not established. Psilocin: specimen-specific percentage not established. Gymnopilin percentage by mass: not established. Approximately 50%: a cultured-cell assay response reduction, NOT mushroom composition or human incidence. Historical 0.0004%: method detection threshold, NOT concentration. Ergosterol, ergosteryl peroxide, cerevisterol, choline and an acetylenic triol: isolated by Kusano et al. in the examined collection.

GYMNOPILIN NEUROPHARMACOLOGY · Evidence and Mechanisms

Scientific status: in-vitro and limited animal-tissue evidence. No confirmed human mechanism of hallucination; no validated human onset, duration, pharmacokinetics, or percentage composition for these specimen photographs.

1. Identity, chemical class and specimen scope

Gymnopilins are bitter polyisoprenoid polyols (polyisoprene-derived molecules bearing hydroxyl groups). Historically related gymnoprenol compounds were isolated from material called G. spectabilis. The names G. spectabilis and G. junonius appear in overlapping older literature; isolate identity must be tied to collection/voucher and analytical method. Individual compounds have variable structures and activities. Chemical presence cannot be established from visual appearance.

Interpretation: gymnopilins are experimentally neuroactive and possible contributors to poisoning, but evidence does not prove they independently generate psychedelic hallucinations.

2. Cellular pathway I — nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (PC12, 2014)

Gymnopilin fractions → nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR, ligand-gated cation channel) inhibition → decreased DMPP-evoked whole-cell current and reduced nicotinic agonist-evoked intracellular Ca²⁺ rises. In the specific acetylcholine assay, calcium-response amplitude decreased by approximately 50%. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel currents were not reduced under the reported conditions.

Level: cultured PC12 cells. ~50% is an assay effect size, not fungal mass fraction, human incidence, or predicted human response. Kayano et al. (2014), PubMed; DOI.

3. Cellular pathway II — PLC / intracellular calcium (DRG cultures, 2012)

Purified gymnopilin → increased intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations in rat dorsal-root-ganglion cultures, including neuronal and non-neuronal cells. In the non-neuronal population, U-73122 (PLC inhibitor) and cyclopiazonic acid (intracellular store Ca²⁺-ATPase inhibitor) suppressed the gymnopilin-evoked response, while extracellular calcium removal did not. Results implicate phospholipase C and calcium release from intracellular stores; the precise initiating receptor was not identified.

Level: isolated rat cell cultures, not a human brain study. Miyazaki et al. (2012), PubMed PMID 22572385.

5. Hallucinations: causal uncertainty and comparison with psilocin

Gymnopilin → nAChR inhibition: cell evidence. Gymnopilin → PLC-associated intracellular Ca²⁺ rise: rat-culture evidence. Gymnopilin → human hallucinations: unverified causal hypothesis. Psilocybin → psilocin → 5-HT2A receptor: established general pharmacology but relevant to a particular specimen only when compound content has been measured.

Do not draw a mechanistic arrow from nicotinic inhibition directly to human hallucinations as established fact. Compounds could interact, collection chemistry can differ and taxonomic misidentification complicates the older literature.

6. Quantitative limitations, safety and simulator requirements

Gymnopilin % dry weight: unestablished here. Verified universal psilocybin % for this taxon: unestablished. Human gymnopilin onset/peak/half-life/duration: unestablished. Human hallucination frequency and individual intoxication risk: unestablished. The 2014 ~50% endpoint belongs only to the experimental PC12 calcium-response assay.

Simulator policy: separately display measured chemistry, cellular mechanism, hypothesized human relevance, and unknowns. No ingestion or identification guidance should be inferred from a 3D shape.

Photograph-to-3D scientific reconstruction protocol

Upcoming imagery will be reviewed before mesh changes; images themselves are not yet attached. Preserve unmodified originals and specimen provenance. Each specimen photo group should contain dorsal cap view, ventral gills, lateral stipe and annulus, cluster in situ, hardwood substrate, ruler/scale and mature/immature stages when available. Record date, substrate tree, locality at a privacy-appropriate level, scale in mm, field versus laboratory image, focal length if known, specimen/voucher ID and any uncertainty.

Reconstruction pipeline: source photo → specimen photo ID → scale calibration → segmented geometry (pileus, lamellae, stipe, annulus, substrate) → texture and shape adjustments → multi-angle visual validation → retained prior model version. A photo cannot independently establish metabolite presence or safe identification.

Safety: Possible poisoning and misidentification. This model is an educational visualization; it does not support ingestion decisions or identification of wild mushrooms.
